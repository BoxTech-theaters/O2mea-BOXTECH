Acoustic treatment for theatres and halls: controlling echo and improving clarity

Acoustic treatment deals with what happens to sound inside a room: how long it lingers after the source stops, how it reflects and whether it reaches the listener clearly. It differs from soundproofing, which stops sound passing between inside and outside. At BoxTech O2mea we design treatment with numbers first, then turn it into coherent interior design, drawing on the absorber reference in the site’s acoustics section.

How we approach acoustic treatment

We start by measuring or calculating the existing or expected reverberation time and comparing it with the target for the use. The gap sets how much absorption is needed, in square metres and type, using Sabine’s formula or more detailed calculations for large rooms.

We then place materials intelligently: the rear wall of theatres and halls usually needs absorption to stop echo returning to the platform, side surfaces near the stage may stay reflective to reinforce sound, and some positions benefit from diffusers that scatter rather than absorb.

Acoustic treatment does not mean covering every wall with foam. Over-treatment makes a room dead and weakens natural sound; under-treatment leaves the echo. Our aim is the balance that fits the use.

Project phases

  4. Supply and installation — Materials with documented performance.
  5. Verification — Post-installation measurement on request.

Materials

Fabric absorbers with mineral wool or polyester fibre cores

Effective at mid and high frequencies.

Perforated or slotted timber panels

A timber look with absorption.

Diffusers

To scatter reflections in theatres and listening rooms.

Bass traps

For small rooms and studios.

Engineering aspects

Reverberation time RT60

The time for sound to decay by 60 dB after the source stops; the target varies with use.

Absorption coefficient

We compare documented NRC or αw values.

Distribution

Positions matter as much as quantity.

Safety

Materials with a fire class suitable for public spaces.

Figures quoted are general guidance from common professional practice; final values for each project are set by its use and the requirements of the competent authorities.

Checklist before you start

  • Is the problem sound transfer (isolation) or internal echo (treatment)?
  • Has existing and target reverberation been measured or calculated?
  • Have flanking paths such as ceilings and ducts been identified?
  • Do materials have tested absorption or isolation values?
  • Is the fire classification suitable for a public space?
  • Is treatment integrated into the interior design?

Common mistakes we help you avoid

  • Using foam to soundproof
  • Covering every wall with absorbers so the room goes dead
  • Neglecting doors and openings in isolation projects
  • Relying on an absorption coefficient without knowing the tested mounting

Requirements in Saudi Arabia

Acoustic materials in public spaces in the Kingdom must meet the approved fire-protection requirements, so we request supplier test certificates and check them against the project specification before procurement.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between acoustic treatment and soundproofing?

Treatment improves sound inside the room and reduces echo; soundproofing stops sound entering or leaving. Many projects need both. Our knowledge hub has a detailed article on the difference.

Can a hall be treated without changing its décor?

Usually yes, with elements that suit the existing design, such as fabric panels in the wall colour or suspended ceilings.

How many panels do I need?

It is calculated from room volume, target reverberation and existing absorption, not from a fixed percentage of wall area.

Is studio foam suitable for halls?

Usually not for public spaces; we prefer materials with a suitable fire class and appearance.
